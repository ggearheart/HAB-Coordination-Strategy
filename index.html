Institutional responsibility for harmful algal blooms

Harmful algal blooms (HABs) do not correspond to the organizational structure of the Water Boards. Because a HAB is an emergent condition of a waterbody rather than a discharged pollutant, responsibility is not vested in any single office; the response is instead assembled from the distinct regulatory and monitoring authorities that each Region, Division, and Office already holds.

A HAB cannot be permitted directly. It is an outcome mediated by controllable drivers — flow, nutrient load, and temperature — and resolved only through sustained monitoring.

Panel 1 · Institutional actors

01Six entities and their distinct authorities

Each entity contributes one distinct regulatory or scientific authority to HAB response. No single entity constitutes a HAB program in isolation; the functional program emerges from the coordinated application of these authorities to a common waterbody.

Regional Water Boards Regional water-quality authority Hold jurisdiction over water-quality protection within their basins through Basin Plans, designated beneficial uses, and water-quality objectives. HABs require extension beyond conventional pollutant-based tools toward monitoring and source-linkage analysis.
Division of Water Rights Flow administration Administer water rights, water use, and minimum-flow requirements, and may compel monitoring. Flow regime and hydraulic residence time are antecedent conditions for most bloom formation.
Division of Drinking Water Drinking-water oversight Regulate and support several thousand public water systems statewide to ensure potable-water safety — the principal responder when cyanotoxins reach a source water or intake.
Division of Water Quality Regulatory criteria & policy Develop the regulatory instruments — water-quality standards, objectives, and prohibitions — that the Regional Boards and, in the Delta, the Division of Water Rights apply to protect beneficial uses.
OIMA Monitoring & notification Coordinate statewide HAB monitoring, directing a partner network to sample, confirm blooms, and issue public notification regarding the hazards of recreational contact with affected waters.
OPEETA Tribal affairs & TBUs The Office of Public Engagement, Equity & Tribal Affairs — the Boards' formal tribal liaison, leading government-to-government engagement with sovereign, federally recognized tribes and coordinating the Tribal Beneficial Use (TBU) process, including the cultural (CUL) use adopted by the Regional Boards into Basin Plans.

Panel 2 · Causal pathway

02From controllable drivers to human exposure

A HAB is not introduced to a waterbody; it proliferates when environmental conditions permit. Those conditions are governed by factors partially within the Boards' regulatory influence: diversion and discharge volumes, nutrient loading, and water temperature and hydraulic residence time. HAB management therefore depends on monitoring to establish the linkage between drivers and bloom occurrence, rather than on a single discharge permit.

Panel 4 · Concurrent activation

04A recurrent reservoir HAB engages every part of our organization

Reservoirs and State Water Project facilities are nodes at which multiple functions coincide: water is stored, conveyed for supply, withdrawn for potable use, and used for recreation. A single bloom therefore engages all six entities concurrently — an interdependency that a pollutant-by-pollutant framework cannot represent.

Panel 5 · Tribal beneficial uses

05Tribal Beneficial Uses and the cultural (CUL) designation

Federally recognized tribes are sovereign governments. The Office of Public Engagement, Equity & Tribal Affairs (OPEETA) serves as the Boards' formal tribal liaison and leads government-to-government engagement. In that capacity OPEETA coordinates and supports the Tribal Beneficial Use (TBU) process, through which uses such as the cultural (CUL) use — collecting material for ceremonies and conducting ceremonies in and near water — are developed and, by the nine Regional Boards, adopted into Basin Plans. The specific uses vary by tribe, community, and place. Once adopted, a designated use is a protected beneficial use, which is why HAB monitoring and notification at these waters is consequential.

Panel 6 · System differentiation

06Freshwater and marine systems are distinct problems

The fourth pathway — dietary exposure through contaminated food — differs materially from the other three. It is concentrated in the marine environment, driven by different causative taxa and toxins, and led primarily by partner agencies. Presenting the two systems together prevents the freshwater model from being over-extended to marine waters.

Status. This is a preliminary (v1) conceptual model intended as a basis for review and revision, not a finished graphic. The six-color assignment is held constant across panels so that entities can be tracked between views. Anticipated revisions: refinement of the lead and supporting designations in Panel 3, addition or renaming of waterbody archetypes in Panel 4, confirmation of the Tribal Beneficial Use roles in Panel 5, and confirmation of marine agency roles in Panel 6.

Role assignments represent a working characterization of typical responsibilities, not a legal delineation of authority. Management of marine seafood toxins in California is led primarily by CDPH, OEHHA, and CDFW. For the waters in which freshwater HABs occur, see the CyanoSafe monitoring effort.
